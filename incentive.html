America and Opium Trade

Hosea Ballou Morse, 1900. The international relations of the Chinese empire.

Prior to the 1830s, America held economically prospered from the International Opium Trade. However, by the end of 1839, opium trade lost its value.

"Americans had lost their strategic position in [opium], American capital no longer played a dominant role in the provision of opium for American consignment, and American vessels were still barred from the carrying trade in opium between India and China"

(Charles C. Stelle, American Trade in Opium to China, 1821-39, 1944)

Opium trade was described as a selfish act executed for profit.

"Let the merchants who traffic in this 'man-destroying merchandize' think of their responsibility to God and abandon it."

(The Chinese Repository, "Remarks on the Opium Trade With China," 1837)

"​​​​​​​You do not desire to promote vice and misery in themselves considered, but you actually prefer the introduction of both, rather than forego your commercial gains!"

(The Chinese Repository, "Remarks on the Opium Trade With China," 1837)

The antagonization of opium drove national reform efforts by framing the drug as a direct threat to moral and social stability. By portraying addiction as corruptive, a public urgency for state intervention was created, legitimizing restriction of morphine.

Xenophobic Fear of Opium

By the late nineteenth century, China experienced a widespread opium addiction crisis, posing major public health and social challenges.

Chinese Opioid Abuse

(The Chinese Repository, "Estimate of the Consumption of Indian Opium," 1838)

This data illustrates how opium addiction was a national crisis, affecting many people in China.

"We shall thus find...one in every five men in the prime of life, or verging to old age, an habitual opium smoker!"

(Elijah Coleman Bridgman, "Abuse of Opium," The Chinese Repository, 1840)

"A Chinese gentle-man of good position, in telling me at Canton that the opium habit was the greatest evil that had ever fallen upon China, said that of the sixteen of his father's family, eleven were addicted to the habit."

(Alfred Syace Dyer, The Great Plague of Asia, 1891)

Western observers increasingly equated opium addiction with Chinese inferiority, fueling xenophobic portrayals of Chinese communities.

"If the Chinese ruin themselves by opium, that is their own affair; why do they not abstain from it?"

(Alfred Syace Dyer, The Great Plague of Asia, 1891)

Racial Stereotypes

"The moralist saw in the Chinese a people...living in filthy places and keeping opium dens of disgust and horror ; a people, however, to be civilized and converted. The politician saw in the Chinese a people dangerous to American institutions."

(Mildred Wellborn, The Events Leading to the Chinese Exclusion Acts, 1912-1913)

"Wherever the Chinese are found there will be the odor of opium."

(C. F. Holder, The Opium Industry in America, 1898)

(W. W. Bode, Searching Chinese immigrants for opium, at San Francisco, 1875)

In the Age of Immigration, Americans viewed Chinese communities as carriers of the contagious opium addiction disease. Opium became racialized as a threat associated with Chinese immigrants, justifying exclusionary policies.

"The Chinaman is beginning to give manifestations of the change from adventurous fortune-hunting to permanent colonization. He is monopolizing 'quarters' in the large cities, bringing out his women, setting up his joss-house, his opium-den, his theatre, his gambling-place, and other institutions of Chinese civilization, which are rapidly reconciling him to America as a home. He is even beginning to bury his dead here..."

(Alfred Syace Dyer, The Great Plague of Asia, 1891)

This fear led U.S. leaders to conclude that domestic restrictions were insufficient, as America's role as a global trade center meant opium production abroad remained a persistent threat, necessitating international suppression.
